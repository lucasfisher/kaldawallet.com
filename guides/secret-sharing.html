Recovery shares from friends and family

Split your backup among people you trust, so a few of them together can bring your wallet back.

Simple

Your recovery phrase is the key to your whole wallet. If you lose it, your money is gone. Secret sharing gives you a safety net.

You can cut your phrase into pieces, called shares, and give one to each person you trust. It's like a treasure map torn into pieces: one piece on its own shows nothing.

You choose how many pieces are needed. For example, 7 people, and any 4 of them together can bring your wallet back if you lose it, or for your family if you pass away.

Set it up in Profile → Backup & security → Secret sharing, and send each share as a private Zcash message to a contact, by email with a password, or as words in a letter. To use shares later, choose Recover with shares on Kalda's welcome screen.

Choose people who won't all get together behind your back: enough of them together could open your wallet. If you think too many shares are no longer safe, move your money to a new wallet.

Detailed

Secret sharing is built on a well-known idea from cryptography, Shamir's secret sharing, published by Adi Shamir in 1979. Kalda's shares follow SLIP-39, a public, open standard that turns Shamir shares into lists of words.

  • Threshold: you pick N people and a number T. Any T shares rebuild the recovery phrase; T − 1 or fewer reveal nothing about it, not even a hint.
  • Words with error detection: each share is written with a fixed word list, so it can be copied by hand, read aloud or kept on paper, and carries a checksum, so a mistyped word is caught instead of producing the wrong wallet.
  • Sets stay together: every share in a set carries the same identifier, so shares from two different sets can't be mixed by accident.

Getting shares to people safely

A private Zcash message is encrypted by the Zcash network itself, so only the recipient can read it. Email and chat apps aren't private, so a share sent that way travels locked with a password; give the password another way, such as in person, by phone or in your will. Words in a letter need nothing more.

No scheme can take shares back once they are given out: making a new set doesn't stop the old one working. And if more than N − T people lose their shares, there aren't enough left to recover. The only way to retire old shares is to move your money to a new wallet.

In depth

Shamir's secret sharing rests on a simple fact about polynomials: two points fix a straight line, three points fix a parabola, and in general T points fix exactly one polynomial of degree T − 1.

Splitting

To share a secret s with threshold T, choose a random polynomial of degree T − 1 whose value at zero is the secret. Each person receives one point on that curve, taken at a different, non-zero x.

f(x) = s + a₁x + a₂x² + … + a₍T−1₎x^(T−1)     share i = (xᵢ, f(xᵢ))

Rebuilding

Any T shares determine the polynomial uniquely, and Lagrange interpolation gives its value at zero, which is the secret:

s = f(0) = Σᵢ yᵢ · Πⱼ≠ᵢ xⱼ / (xⱼ − xᵢ)

Why fewer shares reveal nothing

The arithmetic is done in a finite field (for byte-sized secrets, typically a field of 256 elements), not with ordinary numbers. With only T − 1 points, every possible secret is matched by exactly one polynomial through those points, so each value is equally likely. This is information-theoretic security: it holds no matter how much computing power an attacker has.

In practice, long secrets are split byte by byte with fresh random coefficients each time. Plain Shamir can't tell a wrong share from a right one, so practical schemes add a checksum to each share and a digest of the secret to detect mistakes. And the scheme protects the secret only until T holders combine their shares, so the threshold is a trade-off: high enough to resist collusion, low enough to survive lost shares.
